1787 – Northwest Ordinance

Congress organized the territory that became Indiana. It banned slavery there and declared that schools and education "shall forever be encouraged," which symbolized an early hook for academic freedom.

1816 – First constitution

The 1816 delegates largely copied the Ohio Constitution of 1802 and the Kentucky Constitution of 1799. The document was never put to a popular vote; it took effect June 29, the day the convention adjourned. It already had a free speech clause, and it called for a public school system running from township schools up to a state university.

1851 – Current constitution

Article 1, §9 is still the text courts apply today: no law may restrain "the free interchange of thought and opinion" or the right to speak, write, or print on any subject, but every person is responsible for abusing that right. Section 31 protects assembly and petition. However, it also contained Article 13, which said no Black or mixed-race person could come into or settle in the state, and Black residents already there had to register with their county.

1866 / 1881 – Article 13 struck down, then repealed

The Indiana Supreme Court invalidated Article 13 in Smith v. Moody (1866), the legislature repealed the laws enforcing it in 1867, and the article itself was removed by amendment in 1881. The court held that Article 13 violated the U.S. Constitution's Privileges and Immunities Clause.

1970 – Merit selection

Voters approved a constitutional amendment that year for merit selection of Supreme Court justices and Court of Appeals judges. It took effect January 1, 1972. A seven-member commission nominates three candidates and the governor picks one: three lawyers elected by the bar, three non-lawyers appointed by the governor, and the Chief Justice as chair.

1973 – Hess v. Indiana

The case began with an antiwar demonstration on the Indiana University campus. Hess was convicted under Indiana's disorderly conduct law for shouting a profane line about taking the street later, and the Indiana Supreme Court affirmed. On November 19, 1973, the U.S. Supreme Court reversed. It held that his words amounted at most to advocating illegal action at some indefinite future time, so they did not meet "imminent lawless action" test.

1991 – Barnes v. Glen Theatre

The Court held that Indiana could regulate totally nude dancing without violating the First Amendment, even though that dancing was expressive conduct. The plaintiffs were adult venues in South Bend. Applying the O'Brien test, the plurality found the public indecency law within the state's power and unrelated to suppressing expression.

1993 – Price v. State

The Indiana Supreme Court held that Article 1, §9 protected Price's protest of police conduct. Because it was political speech, the state could not punish it without identifiable harm comparable to a tort injury. Commentators called this a bold framework that made political speech a "core value" under the state constitution.

2015 – Religious Freedom Restoration Act

Mike Pence signed RFRA on March 26, 2015, and critics argued it let businesses discriminate against LGBT people. Angie's List, Salesforce, the NCAA, and Indianapolis's mayor publicly opposed it. About a week later Pence signed Senate Enrolled Act 50, the "fix," which made explicit that the law did not authorize discrimination against protected classes.

2022 – HEA 1190

The governor signed it on March 15, 2022 (Public Law 145). It bans state colleges from designating outdoor areas where protected expression is prohibited, and from discriminating against religious, political, or ideological student groups. It also requires published expressive-activity policies and gives students a right to sue. It passed both chambers unanimously. Indiana University, Purdue, Ball State, and the Attorney General's office all testified in support.

2024–2026 – Three separate threads

(a) SEA 202, the "intellectual diversity" law.

  • It took effect July 1, 2024. It ties tenure and promotion to fostering free inquiry and exposing students to a variety of political or ideological frameworks. It also requires post-tenure review every five years and a complaint system for students and employees.
  • It bans mandatory diversity statements in hiring, admissions, and promotion.
  • Litigation: the first ACLU suit was filed in May 2024 on behalf of two Purdue Fort Wayne professors. A second suit followed in September 2024 with IU and Purdue faculty, and the district court dismissed it in July 2025 for lack of concrete harm.
  • On August 13, 2026, the Seventh Circuit affirmed in McDonald v. Trustees of Indiana University, holding the professors lacked standing because they showed no credible threat of enforcement. The ruling leaves open whether the law itself is constitutional. Judge Easterbrook's concurrence argued that professors' classroom speech "has not been regulated; it has been hired."

(b) IU protest policy.

  • During the April 2024 pro-Palestine encampment in Dunn Meadow, police arrested 57 protesters after a last-minute rule change on temporary structures.
  • IU's Expressive Activity Policy took effect August 1, 2024. It banned expressive activity from 11 p.m. to 6 a.m., and violators risked suspension, expulsion, or termination. The ACLU sued that month. This is against the 2022 HEA 1190.
  • Later, IU revised the policy in November 2024.
  • Judge Richard L. Young then granted a preliminary injunction, finding the policy likely restricted far more speech than needed for public safety. That ruling came on May 29, 2025.

(c) HEA 1001 (2025 state budget).

  • The budget bill added faculty productivity reviews, required syllabi to be posted online, eliminated low-enrollment degrees, and declared faculty governance "advisory" only.
  • IU alumni had directly elected three trustees since 1891. The bill's late amendments gave the governor the power to appoint all nine. Gov. Mike Braun removed all three alumni-elected trustees in June 2025 and appointed conservatives Jim Bopp and Sage Steele. In July 2026, a state special judge granted the state summary judgment, upholding the change.

The future is still not clear for the state, especially whether AF to what extent will still be granted. By June 30, 2025, public colleges had eliminated, suspended, or merged more than 400 degree programs, including 249 across IU's campuses. The Commission for Higher Education later voted to act on more than 1,000 low-enrollment programs, and a new review of low-wage degrees under SEA 199 begins July 1,2026.
